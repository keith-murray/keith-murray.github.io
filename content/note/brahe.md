During the spring of 2024, I took on this odd project of [digitizing anecdotes from the _Collected Works of Warren S. McCulloch_](https://github.com/keith-murray/warren-mcculloch/). I felt compelled to digitize these anecdotes because they're filled with perspectives from a time where modeling cognition in the nervous system was controversial. Despite the research program becoming increasingly fashionable, even to the point where whole departments are predicated on it, many of the perspectives still apply today. My favorite is the following, written by Jerome Lettvin, a close friend of McCulloch, and taken from page 336 of the collected works, under an article titled "Warren as a Teacher":
> [!quote]
> It is a pity that the science of the nervous system, which is the most backward of all sciences in having no central theory at all, should hold continually to a regurgitation of data without the application of any imagination whatsoever. Certainly no theory can arise from data alone. One must bring to the data an image, however wrong, of the way things are arranged in order to be able to set an idea of a theory that is proper. To approach the whole business of the most complex system known to us, that of the brain, in a manner that supposes that if enough mounds of data are built up a theory will somehow or other emerge, is to go contrary to the history of science and to go contrary to all the practices that one observes in other fields. Wrong guesses and wrong theories may be much more important than the acquisition of new data, and Warren continually practiced theorizing and taught his students the practice of theorizing as a way of designing proper experiments.

When I first read this quote around November of 2022, about 4 years ago, I was enthralled by Lettvin's ornery voice, leading me to unquestioningly accept the sentiment's truth. In fact, this point is supported by numerous examples in the history of science where theory led to the design of successful experiments:

- Einstein's theory of relativity came before the [1919 Eddington experiment](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Eddington_experiment).
- Upon seeing the [Madagascar Christmas Orchid](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angraecum_sesquipedale), Darwin reasoned, via his theory of natural selection, that there must be a giant moth with an incredibly long tongue to pollinate the orchid. 21 years after Darwin died, researchers found[^1] a moth with a [12-inch tongue](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Xanthopan).
- The [theory of ring attractor networks](https://doi.org/10.1523/JNEUROSCI.16-06-02112.1996) for keeping track of head direction came before [its discovery in fruit flies](https://doi.org/10.1126/science.aal4835).

There are more examples, but these three highlight the effectiveness in all scientific fields of using theory to make predictions about the natural world. However, in spite of this effectiveness and the ornery Lettvin, theory driven science is not the only means of conducting science. Moreover, the whole scientific revolution, in whose wake we find ourselves in today, was kickstarted by a dataset collected without any theory.

The unsung hero of the scientific revolution is a Danish astronomer by the name of Tycho Brahe, known for making the most precise naked-eye astronomical observations in history. During the 16th century, there were two predominant models of the solar system: the Ptolemaic model that posited the Earth to be the center and the Copernican model that posited the sun to be the center. Ptolemy had argued that the Earth was the center of the universe on philosophical grounds while Copernicus had argued that the Sun was the center on geometrical grounds. Sympathetic to both perspectives, Tycho formulated his own model arguing that the Earth was the center of the universe, the sun and the moon orbited around the Earth, and everything else orbited around the Sun.

However, oddly critical to the scientific revolution, Tycho did not design an experiment to test his model or postulate any phenomenon that would prove his model or disprove others. Instead, he simply resolved to collecting high-quality data for 20 years at his remote observatory Uraniborg. His conviction[^2] was that the Ptolemaic and Copernican models were wrong in ways that densely sampled, precise data will reveal. Tycho didn't specify how this data would "prove the haters wrong"; he only argued that only by collecting such data could we prove them wrong.

The [[note/irony.md | ironic]] part is that Kepler, already a committed Copernican, used Tycho's own data to discover that the planets move in ellipses around the Sun, producing a heliocentric system far more accurate and parsimonious than Ptolemy's, Copernicus's, or Tycho's own model. This ironic lesson in the scientific revolution tells us two things wrong with Lettvin's perspective:

1. Tycho's data ended up being far more important than Tycho's incorrect theory.
2. Theorizing is not the only means of designing a proper experiment. Sometimes all you need is high-quality data.

Yet, Lettvin's point isn't entirely wrong. Looking at the role Kepler played, Kepler came to Tycho's data with his own theory, namely that Copernicus was right. Suppose that there were no heliocentric models of the universe. Could any scientist have looked at Tycho's data with [innocent eyes](https://gershmanlab.com/pubs/innocent_eye.pdf) and have come away with the heliocentric model of the solar system, much less Kepler's laws of planetary motion? [This seems wildly improbable](https://doi.org/10.1371/journal.pcbi.1005268), if not impossible.

Combining the best parts of Lettvin's quote and Tycho's data, we get the following:
> [!info] Collecting data doesn't need theory, but understanding data does
>
> Scientists don't need theory before collecting data. However, theory and models are needed to understand that data once collected.

Moreover, in some cases there could be quite some time before the necessary theory is even available to make sense of that data. In the context of neuroscience, I fear that there may be many years, if not decades, before the data that we've worked so hard to collect today will make any sense.

[^1]: Finding an insect isn't necessarily an experiment, but it certainly gave entomologists something to look for.
[^2]: This conviction can be regarded as a theory of sorts. Albeit, it's a theory of methodology, not of the natural world.
